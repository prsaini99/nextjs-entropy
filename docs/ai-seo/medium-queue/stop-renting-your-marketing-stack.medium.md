# Your Marketing Stack Is a Landlord, and You're the Tenant

We build marketing software for a living, so a founder asked us something last week that deserves a longer answer than the call allowed: "Why does my marketing budget feel like rent?"

Because it is rent.

The average enterprise marketing team now runs 91 tools. Gartner's own research says roughly half of licensed martech features never get used. You're paying for an apartment where half the rooms are locked — and every year the landlord raises the rate, changes the furniture, and occasionally demolishes a wing you were living in. More than a thousand martech products disappeared from the landscape last year alone. When a SaaS vendor sunsets a tool, your workflow dies with their roadmap, not yours.

## The pricing model is designed against your growth

Look at how most marketing SaaS charges you: per contact, per seat, per send. The better your marketing performs — the bigger your list, the larger your team — the more you pay. Your costs scale with your *success*, not with the vendor's costs. Growing your email list from 50,000 to 500,000 contacts costs the vendor almost nothing in storage. It costs you a tier upgrade.

When we built AtoEmail, our own marketing automation platform, we made one decision that still surprises people: no per-contact pricing at all. Visual journey builder, high-volume campaigns, a unified inbox, AI steps inside workflows — and your list can grow tenfold without your bill noticing. That wasn't generosity; it was just honest cost modeling. It made us realize how much of the SaaS pricing you accept is habit, not economics.

## The build-vs-buy math changed and nobody sent a memo

Five years ago, "just use HubSpot" was usually right. Building your own marketing infrastructure meant a year of engineering before the first campaign went out. That world is gone. AI-accelerated development — with senior engineers reviewing everything that ships — compressed those timelines dramatically. A focused first release of a custom system now ships in 6–12 weeks, typically in the ₹5–15 Lakh range. Larger platforms run ₹15–40 Lakh and up.

Compare that to a mid-market stack quietly burning the same amount *every year* in licenses — with roughly half of it going to features nobody opens.

Industry surveys now show about one in four new martech capabilities are built in-house rather than bought. That number was unthinkable in 2020. The memo just hasn't reached most marketing departments yet.

## What owning actually looks like

Owning your stack doesn't mean rebuilding Salesforce for sport. It means the workflows that are *core to how you win* run on software shaped around you:

| You probably rent | Owning it looks like |
|---|---|
| Email/automation platform priced per contact | Your own journeys engine — unlimited contacts, AI steps, your data |
| A CRM your team fights with | A CRM built around how your team actually sells — ours captures WhatsApp, calls and email in one timeline, with AI scoring that tells reps who to call next |
| Ad dashboards across 15 logins | One ad-ops platform with AI analysis on every ad, publishing straight through the platform APIs |
| Gut-feel creative decisions | An analysis pipeline that reads emotion, voice, pacing, script and predicted attention on every video ad before media spend |

We know this list is real because it's our own stack. Our BD team closes deals on a CRM we built. Our campaigns run on our own automation engine. Our AI call center answers in 11 languages. We didn't build these to sell a philosophy — we built them because renting was worse.

## When renting is still right

Honesty matters here: don't build your accounting software. Don't build video conferencing. Rent everything that is *not* how you differentiate. The build decision applies to the systems where your process itself is the advantage — your lead engine, your customer data, your creative intelligence. If a tool's default workflow fits you fine, keep renting it. If you've hired people whose job is fighting the tool, that's the signal.

## The question to ask your own stack

Total up your annual SaaS spend on marketing tools. Then ask: if this number were an engineering budget instead, what would we own by next year — and what would we still be renting forever?

For a surprising number of teams, the honest answer to that question is the moment the math flips.
